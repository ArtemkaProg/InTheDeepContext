# InTheDeep --- Geological Regions and Environments

**Status:** Working framework, not a final region list\
**Depends on:** [`WORLD_FOUNDATION.md`](WORLD_FOUNDATION.md)

This document proposes a practical vocabulary for building a connected
ocean. The examples are candidate ingredients, not a promise that every
one will appear in the final game.

## 2. Candidate geological settings

### 2.1 Abyssal ocean basin and plains

**What it is:** Broad deep-seafloor areas, often covered by fine
sediment. "Flat" does not mean featureless; low hills, nodules, isolated
rocks, traces, and small changes in sediment can provide visual and
investigative interest.

**Visual reference:** [NOAA --- What are abyssal
plains?](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/what-are-abyssal-plains/)
and [NOAA/USGS --- Abyssal Plain
Nodules](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/multimedia/okeanos-explorations-ex2104-features-nodule-media-abyssal-nodules/).

**Potential gameplay use:** Long-distance navigation, subtle clues,
isolated finds, and a strong sense of scale. Avoid filling the plain
with constant landmarks just to prevent emptiness; use negative space
intentionally.

### 2.2 Mid-ocean ridge and basaltic terrain

**What it is:** A broad tectonic setting associated with seafloor
spreading. Volcanic rock, including pillow lava, can form rugged
terrain. Hydrothermal activity may occur in suitable places but should
not be assumed everywhere along a ridge.

**Visual reference:** [NOAA --- Pillow Tube / pillow
lava](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/multimedia/okeanos-explorations-22voyage-to-the-ridge-gallery-media-dive07-pillow-tube/)
and [NOAA --- Hydrothermal Vents and
Volcanoes](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/education/hydrothermal-vents-volcanoes/).

**Potential gameplay use:** Broken relief, exposed rock, crevices, steep
local drops, mineral deposits, and occasional hydrothermal areas. Use
distinct silhouettes and rock structure rather than simply making the
ground red or glowing.

### 2.3 Seamounts and volcanic chains

**What it is:** Underwater mountains that may have volcanic origins.
Their rock, sediment cover, slope, and biological communities can change
with geological age, currents, and depth.

**Visual reference:** [NOAA --- Pillow lava field ending at an abrupt
cliff](https://www.noaa.gov/noaa-collections/photo-library/expn5936jpg)
and [NOAA --- lightly sedimented pillow
lavas](https://www.noaa.gov/noaa-collections/photo-library/expn8359jpg).

**Potential gameplay use:** Large-scale navigation landmarks, exposed
ledges, sediment shadows, sheltered areas, and difficult routes around
steep terrain.

### 2.4 Continental margin and submarine canyons

**What it is:** A continental margin may transition from shelf to slope
and deeper basin. Submarine canyons cut into the seafloor and can
channel sediment and water movement. Their geology varies with region.

**Visual reference starting point:** [NOAA Ocean Exploration --- ocean
exploration imagery and expeditions](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/).
Search the site for "submarine canyon", "continental slope", "sediment
wall", and "turbidity current".

**Potential gameplay use:** Vertical routes, confined passages,
overhangs, debris deposits, difficult navigation, and places where
current or visibility changes along the route.

**Research note:** Add a specific photographic reference before defining
the final canyon style. Do not use a generic fantasy canyon as the
scientific baseline.

### 2.5 Trenches and subduction margins

**What it is:** Deep depressions associated with one tectonic plate
descending beneath another. A trench is a large geological structure,
not simply a very deep crack. Sediment, slope failures, and local
geology vary along its length.

**Potential gameplay use:** Extreme depth, steep walls, restricted
routes, landslide deposits, and high-risk expeditions. A trench should
feel like a large geological system rather than a narrow decorative
fissure.

**Research note:** The Tonga Trench is a useful real-world reference for
trench scale and subduction context. Research bathymetry and geology
before choosing its game equivalent.

### 2.6 Hydrothermal vent fields

**What it is:** Seawater circulates through fractured crust, is heated
by geological sources, reacts with rocks, and emerges carrying dissolved
chemicals. Some vents form mineral chimneys; the exact appearance
depends on local chemistry and conditions.

**Visual reference:** [NOAA --- Hydrothermal Vents and
Volcanoes](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/education/hydrothermal-vents-volcanoes/)
and [NOAA --- High-Temperature Hydrothermal Vent
Field](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/multimedia/high-temperature-hydrothermal-vent-field/).

**Potential gameplay use:** Heat, chemically distinctive water, unusual
mineral structures, specialized organisms, and difficult observation.
Avoid making every vent field a field of giant black chimneys; vent
systems vary.

### 2.7 Cold seeps

**What it is:** Places where methane-rich or other chemically distinct
fluids seep from the seafloor. They can support chemosynthetic
communities, including mussels or tube worms. They are not the same
process as hot hydrothermal vents.

**Visual reference:** [NOAA --- Mussels at a cold
seep](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/multimedia/okeanos-explorations-ex1903-background-seeps-media-mound/)
and [NOAA --- Cold Seeps
overview](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/education/cold-seeps/).

**Potential gameplay use:** Local biological abundance in otherwise
sparse areas, chemical clues, mineral crusts, and discoveries that
reward careful observation.

## 3. Surface materials and textures

The material system should describe what is physically present, not
which biome the player is in.

### Sediments

-   **Clay and fine silt:** Smooth-looking deposits at a distance;
    subtle surface variation and tracks at close range.
-   **Fine sand:** Grainy surface, ripples where currents permit them,
    local accumulations.
-   **Coarse sand and gravel:** More visible particles and mixed sizes;
    may collect in higher-energy locations.
-   **Biogenic sediment:** Material derived partly from the remains of
    organisms; composition varies with depth and environment.
-   **Rock fragments and talus:** Angular or rounded debris depending on
    source and transport history.

### Bedrock and crust

-   **Basalt:** Common in oceanic crust; may appear as pillow lava,
    fractured flows, broken blocks, or weathered outcrops depending on
    geological context.
-   **Other igneous rocks:** Use only where the selected geological
    setting supports them.
-   **Sedimentary rock:** Layering and fracture patterns should reflect
    the chosen formation and history.
-   **Mineral crusts and precipitates:** Local features near suitable
    chemical or hydrothermal processes, not a universal decoration.

### Surface-cover rules

-   Sediment should accumulate where terrain and currents make that
    plausible.
-   Steep or exposed areas may reveal bedrock; depressions may collect
    finer material.
-   Organisms should depend on substrate and environmental conditions.
-   Repetition should be broken by natural variation in scale,
    orientation, coverage, and local deposits, not by random colour
    changes.

## 4. Environments emerge from overlapping conditions

For each area, consider the combination of:

-   depth and ambient pressure;
-   light availability and artificial illumination;
-   temperature;
-   current direction and strength;
-   water visibility and suspended particles;
-   sediment supply and grain size;
-   bedrock type and exposure;
-   local chemistry;
-   available food sources and biological interactions.

These variables do not need to be simulated with full scientific
precision. They should, however, be consistent enough that the world
looks and plays as if they matter.

## 5. Environmental danger

Hazards should be grounded in the location and, where possible,
foreshadowed.

Possible categories to research and evaluate:

-   **Current-driven:** strong or changing currents in constrictions,
    around terrain, or near specific flow features.
-   **Terrain-driven:** steep walls, overhangs, narrow gaps, unstable
    rubble, and difficult ascent/descent routes.
-   **Sediment-driven:** suspended sediment or local events that reduce
    visibility.
-   **Thermal or chemical:** localized near relevant geological sources,
    not spread arbitrarily across an area.
-   **Navigation-driven:** poor visibility, confusing terrain, few safe
    routes, or difficulty identifying landmarks.
-   **Equipment and depth-related:** pressure and other physical limits
    that apply consistently to the player's vehicle or equipment.

For every proposed hazard, document: its cause, warning signs, area of
effect, player response, failure consequence, and how it supports
exploration rather than merely punishing the player.

## 6. Transition rules

A transition should be explained by one or more changing conditions.

Examples: - fine sediment gradually thins as exposed rock becomes more
common; - a plain slopes into a canyon, with debris accumulating at its
base; - a ridge becomes steeper and more fractured near a volcanic
feature; - biological cover changes as substrate, current, depth, or
chemistry changes; - visibility decreases locally where sediment is
suspended.

Abrupt changes are allowed when a real boundary explains them, such as a
cliff, fault scarp, lava flow boundary, or sharp habitat edge. Avoid
arbitrary borders between entire colour-coded zones.
